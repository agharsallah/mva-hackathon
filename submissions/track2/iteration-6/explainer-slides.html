Chapter 1 · The setting

Your body is a city of about 37 trillion tiny workers

Each worker is a cell. To grow, heal and replace old ones, cells make copies of themselves: one cell splits into two.

🏙️
Think of a city that has to keep building new workers every second. Each new worker must get a complete instruction manual or it will do its job badly.

Press the button and watch a single cell become many. Each split doubles the count.

1 cell

After about 45 rounds of doubling you would pass the number of cells in a human body.

Chapter 2 · The instruction manual

Inside every cell: 46 instruction books

The manual is called DNA. It is split into 46 volumes called chromosomes, in 23 pairs: one of each pair from mum, one from dad.

A gene is one recipe inside a book. Each recipe tells the cell how to build one machine, called a protein.

📚
DNA = the whole library · chromosome = one book · gene = one recipe · protein = the machine the recipe builds.

Before a cell splits, it photocopies all 46 books. Then it has 92, and it must hand exactly 46 to each new cell. That handing-over is where our story happens.

Chapter 3 · The hero of the story

Meet BubR1, the cell's seatbelt inspector

When a cell splits, ropes pull each copied book to opposite sides. Before the ropes pull, a protein called BubR1 walks down the line and checks that every book is clipped to a rope.

🎢
Like the attendant on a roller-coaster who checks every seatbelt before the ride starts. If one belt is open, the ride waits.

Try it: run the split with the inspector on, then switch it off. Watch which new cell ends up with too many or too few books.

Inspector on: both new cells get exactly the right set.

Chapter 4 · The illness

A weak inspector means cells with the wrong number of books

Scientists call this mistake aneuploidy. When the inspector is weak in the whole body, the result is a rare condition called MVA (mosaic variegated aneuploidy). "Mosaic" means some cells are fine and some are not, like a patchwork.

📏

Slow growth

Cells with wrong book counts work badly or die, so the body grows more slowly. The head is often small (microcephaly).

💪

Weak muscles

Tissue that needs a lot of healthy cells, like muscle, is affected. Our child has muscle wasting.

⚠️

Cancer risk

Cells with scrambled books can grow out of control. Children with MVA can get cancers early, so safety matters a lot.

Chapter 5 · What went wrong in this child

Two copies of the recipe, and both are damaged

Everyone has two copies of the BubR1 recipe (the gene is called BUB1B): one from mum, one from dad. One good copy is usually enough. This child inherited a different mistake on each copy.

🛑 Copy 1: a stop sign halfway

A single letter change turns a normal word into "STOP" in the middle of the recipe. The cell has a quality check that notices recipes stopping too early and shreds them (nonsense-mediated decay). So copy 1 makes almost nothing.

✏️ Copy 2: one wrong letter

One letter is swapped, so one building block of the protein is different (a missense change). The recipe is still read, and the cell still builds the inspector, but with one wrong part, in the section called the pseudokinase domain.

So the child's whole supply of inspectors comes from copy 2. If we want to help, copy 2 is the only lever left.

Chapter 6 · Double-checking the diagnosis

A "twin" among 76,699 mice

The DNA pointed at BUB1B. But we wanted a second, independent check that did not use the DNA at all.

Doctors describe symptoms with a standard list of terms (HPO terms). The child has 8 of them. Scientists have described 76,699 kinds of genetically changed mice the same way. We asked a computer: which mouse looks most like this child?

top 1%

The best-matching group included a mouse that makes only about 10% of the normal inspector (the Bub1b "hypomorph" mouse). It ranked #758 of 76,699.

Why it matters: the child's body behaves like "too little inspector", not "no inspector at all". Having zero is lethal before birth, so a weak copy 2 fits.

Chapter 7 · How a recipe becomes a machine

A protein is a chain of beads that folds into a shape

The cell reads the recipe and strings together beads called amino acids. BubR1 is a chain of 1,050 beads. The chain then folds itself into a precise 3D shape, like origami. The shape is what lets it do its job.

🦢
A sheet of paper is useless until folded into a crane. One wrong crease can make the whole crane collapse.

In the child, one of the 1,050 beads is the wrong type, and it sits buried deep inside the folded core, not on the surface.

Chapter 8 · The question everything depends on

Does the typo make the inspector crumple, or make it lazy?

A wrong bead can break a protein in two very different ways. Click each one:

Chapter 9 · Asking a computer

We scored all 19,950 possible typos

Each of the 1,050 beads could be swapped for 19 other kinds: 1,050 × 19 = 19,950 possible one-letter typos. A computer model called ThermoMPNN looked at a predicted 3D shape of BubR1 and estimated how much each typo weakens the fold.

The score is in kcal/mol. Think of it as "how much glue was lost". Bigger number = shakier fold.

+1.55

The child's typo. It is more damaging than 87 out of every 100 possible typos (87.3rd percentile).

Chapter 10 · Grading the computer

Can we trust the computer? We gave it an exam

Other families with MVA have published 8 BubR1 typos where a lab measured the answer: 5 crumple, 3 stay stable. We hid the answers and let the computer guess.

The grade is called AUROC. Here is the idea as a game: take one "crumple" typo and one "stable" typo. Does the computer give the crumple one the bigger score? Do this for every pair. 0.5 = coin flip, 1.0 = perfect.

8 typos give only 5 × 3 = 15 pairs.

0.60

On the 8 BubR1 cases. Slightly better than a coin flip, but with so few examples the true grade could be anywhere from 0.13 to 1.00. Too few to decide.

Chapter 11 · A fairer exam

A much bigger exam: 18,825 measured typos in 11 other proteins

Labs have measured, typo by typo, which changes make other proteins disappear from cells. We took 11 such proteins, kept only typos buried in the core (like the child's), and asked the same computer.

0.76

Typical grade across the 11 proteins. The computer does spot fold-breaking typos.

31–88%

Chance that a typo like the child's makes a new protein disappear. Proteins differ too much to narrow it.

🌦️
A weather app that is right most days in 11 cities still can't promise rain in a 12th city it has never seen. It can only say "somewhere between 31% and 88%".

So we kept 55/45. The computer narrowed things, but it can't answer the question for this child. Only a measurement in the child's own cells can.

Chapter 13 · What could help

Two families of ideas, one for each answer

We searched public databases of drugs, lab experiments and mouse studies: 52 candidates in total. Two kinds of evidence rose to the top, and they match the two possible answers to the big question.

If it crumples

🛡️ Protect the inspector: NMN / vitamin B3-type boosters

In mice with a weak inspector, these boosters raised a helper protein (SIRT2) that shields BubR1 from being thrown away. The mice lived about twice as long. They work on the protein's surface, so they don't need a pocket at the typo.

If it is lazy

🧹 Clean up the damage: dasatinib

A weak inspector leaves behind worn-out "zombie" cells (senescent cells). In mice, clearing those cells eased the damage. Dasatinib is an approved drug that clears such cells in research settings.

The trust dial

One lab screen found two everyday pills that raised the inspector by about 15%, but in a single run whose own control check failed. How much should that count against a whole-animal result? Turn the dial and see who wins.

Trust in the one-off screen: 0.25

We set it low (0.25). The winner only changes if you trust the screen above 0.69.

Chapter 14 · Safety first

For a fragile child, what not to give matters just as much

DO NOT

Drugs that block cell division

A whole class of cancer drugs works by jamming the very checkpoint the inspector runs. In cells like the child's, 33 of 34 make things worse. Excluded.

MONITOR

Vincristine

A common chemotherapy. Flagged twice: by the child's DNA and by the child's existing muscle weakness, a known side effect. If ever needed, watch closely.

ORDER A TEST

5 drug-handling genes

The child's DNA file covers only 20 of 1,223 positions that decide how the body processes drugs (1.6%). So we don't guess: we ask for a small, standard 5-gene test.

Chapter 15 · The answer: a test, not a guess

Stop the factory and watch the inspector disappear

The lab test is called a cycloheximide chase. Take a few skin cells from the child (and from a parent for comparison). Add a chemical that stops the cell from making new proteins. Then measure how much inspector is left after 0, 2, 4 and 8 hours.

🧊
Like turning off the ice machine and timing how fast the ice cubes melt. Fast melting means the cubes were fragile.

A second dish with a recycling-bin blocker (MG132) checks that the disappearance really comes from the bin.
